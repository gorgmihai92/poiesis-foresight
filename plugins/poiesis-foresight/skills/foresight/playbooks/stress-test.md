# Stress test: one option, every scenario

A concept, a decision or a plan is read against each scenario in turn, to see where it holds, where it needs
changing and where it stops making sense.

## Read first
Notes in `research/notes` of the plugin's repository.
- `go-science-2024-futures-toolkit`: its Policy Stress-testing section, read in full (p. 79–84). Practitioner
  guidance; no test of the method is reported. The steps below adapt its four steps (Open Government Licence
  v3.0). It calls a single session "a rough and ready process" (p. 84).
- `phadnis-2015-scenario-planning`: read in part (p. 1–4). One scenario read alone pulls judgment toward itself.

## Needs
- **Scenarios with ids** (`playbooks/scenarios.md`), or trends or events to test against. With none, say so and
  offer the choices: build scenarios first, test against the two or three drivers that matter most, or leave it
  out.
- **The option, stated plainly:** what it is, what it is for, and what it assumes. A concept from poiesis-frontier
  brings its **Assumption**; a decision brings its goal.

## Steps (toolkit, p. 81–83)
1. **Recall each scenario** in a few lines: its axes, what is ordinary there.
2. **State the option and its goal.** More than one option can be tested; each gets a row.
3. **For each scenario in turn, suppose the world is as described** and decide, with the reason in one line,
   whether the option is:
   - **robust:** it works there as it is;
   - **in need of modification:** it could work there if changed, and say how;
   - **redundant:** it has no purpose there, or cannot work.
4. **Put the results in one table,** options down the side and scenarios across the top (p. 83).
5. **Read the table** (p. 81):
   - robust in every scenario: carry it forward;
   - robust in some: a contingency for those conditions, or change it to hold more widely;
   - robust in none: revise it;
   - and the changes each scenario asked for: do several scenarios ask for the same one?

## Rules
- "don’t change the scenario to make the policy or option work better" (toolkit, p. 83). Change the option.
- The reason comes from the scenario's own logic, and names the feature of that world it rests on.
- The risk the toolkit names is defending a favoured option (p. 80). Test the option you prefer first and hardest,
  and ask for the scenario in which it fails.
- Every option meets every scenario. An option judged in one scenario only is not tested (Phadnis et al. 2015,
  p. 1).
- A stress test says where an option holds in imagined worlds. It does not say the option will work, and it is no
  substitute for an experiment. For that, poiesis-frontier designs one.

## Then
- Each "needs modification" that depends on something uncertain becomes a signpost (`playbooks/signposts.md`).
- A verdict on the option goes through `playbooks/challenge.md`.
