## What it says

### The frame
- It holds 12 tools (p. 2), ordered under three questions: "WHAT is changing?", "SO WHAT for our futures?" and
  "NOW WHAT do we do?" (p. 9, 10). What is changing: Delphi, Seven Questions, Horizon Scanning, Three Horizons,
  Driver Mapping. So what: SWOT, Scenarios, Visioning, Futures Wheels. Now what: Policy Stress-testing,
  Roadmapping, Backcasting (p. 10).
- A futures project starts from a Scoping Question that sets its breadth, its timeframe and its extent, and
  every exercise is framed by it (p. 6).
- Futures thinking is set apart from forecasting models, which project past trends on a fixed view of how the
  system works (p. 4).
- On proof of effect: "Proving after the event that these impacts were a result of the Futures work will be
  hard" (p. 7).
- Most futures mix appealing and disagreeable features; a discussion drifting to utopia or dystopia is turned by
  asking for the upside or the downside (p. 4, 8).

### Horizon scanning (p. 34–41)
- Five steps: recruit the scanning group; identify sources; gather the scans and store them in an organised
  structure; analyse the scans; write up the results (p. 37).
- Sources beyond the organisation's usual ones, and not only in English (p. 37–38).
- Each scan says what it is about, how it relates to the policy area, and why the scanner thinks it matters; it
  carries metadata such as date, author, source link, category, and a first assessment of timescale and
  importance (p. 38).
- Tips: read the original source; link to it; count the scans in each category so that one, such as technology,
  does not dominate (p. 39). Include ideas "even if they may seem irrelevant at first" (p. 39).
- Scans can be grouped by a fixed framework or by the themes that emerge from them, a "Natural Agenda" (p. 39).
- Analysis: identify the signals of change (established trends, expected developments, newly emerging issues,
  new risks and opportunities, possible future events) and place each on an importance and impact matrix (p. 39).
- Write-up: say how new each driver is to the organisation and whether current plans already take it into
  account; select "perhaps four to eight drivers"; include the high-risk ones and also the low-probability,
  high-impact ones (p. 40).
- What to watch for: "Missing out important changes if you have not scanned widely enough" (p. 35).
- A weak signal, in the glossary: "early warning signs of change, intuitively thought to be significant"
  (p. 115).

### Driver mapping (p. 49–54)
- Three steps: introduce the categories; brainstorm the drivers; map them (p. 51).
- Categories such as PESTLE: political, economic, social, technological, legal and regulatory, environmental
  (p. 51).
- A driver is written as a short phrase that names the direction of change: "Sustained economic growth in
  China" is clearer than "The Economy" (p. 52).
- Drivers are mapped on importance against certainty. More important and more certain: prioritise and act on
  now. More important and more uncertain: research further or use in scenarios. Less important and more
  uncertain: track, since they may grow in significance. Less important and more certain: park (p. 53).
- From the two "more important" quadrants, pick 3 to 5 drivers that matter most (p. 53).
- A critical uncertainty is a driver that is important and uncertain in its outcome; critical uncertainties are
  the input for scenario axes (p. 54, 113).
- What to watch for: the group may not have seen a wide enough range of drivers; a pre-researched list or
  outside experts help (p. 50).

### Scenarios (p. 61–69, 108–111)
- "Scenarios are not plans or predictions of the future" (p. 62). No single scenario will come true; parts of
  several will (p. 63).
- Five steps: develop the axes of uncertainty; describe the world in each scenario; name the scenarios; present
  and discuss; write up (p. 64).
- An axis is a continuum between two opposite outcomes of one uncertain driver; the two ends are extremes, not
  good and bad (p. 64). Start from at least ten critical uncertainties (p. 64).
- The two axes must be independent of each other, so that the four scenarios differ (p. 65).
- Names should capture each scenario's essence and must not be biased toward the one preferred (p. 67).
- Horizon: at least five years, generally ten to twenty (p. 63).
- For each scenario, afterwards: what it means for the stakeholders, and what three recommendations follow if
  one assumes one is in that future (p. 68–69).
- Two other ways to build a set: four standard archetypes (continued growth, decline and collapse, disciplined
  society, transformation), credited to Dator 2009; and morphological analysis, which combines the outcomes of
  several uncertainties and rules out the combinations that could not occur together (p. 109–110).
- Internally consistent, in the glossary: a narrative that does "not include events that cannot happen within
  the scenario logic" (p. 114).

### Futures wheels (p. 75–78)
- Six steps: write the event, trend, issue or strategy in the centre; describe that future as if it has already
  happened; add the immediate (primary) impacts in the first ring, finishing them before going on; add the
  secondary impacts of each primary impact in the second ring, joined to their parent; continue to the longer
  term; debrief and combine the wheels of several groups (p. 77).
- A category model such as PESTLE helps to consider different kinds of impact (p. 77).
- What to watch for: "This is a speculative tool: all inputs are based in an as yet unknown future" (p. 76).
- It is meant to surface surprises, disruptions and backlashes as well as opportunities, including unintended
  consequences (p. 76, 77).

### Policy stress-testing (p. 79–84)
- Also called wind tunnelling: testing policy, strategy or project options against a set of scenarios, trends or
  events (p. 79).
- Four steps: introduce the scenarios; introduce or create the options; test every option against every
  scenario; review the findings (p. 81).
- In each scenario the group imagines the world is as described and decides whether each option is robust,
  redundant or in need of modification, and explains why (p. 83).
- "don’t change the scenario to make the policy or option work better" (p. 83).
- Results go in a table: options down the side, scenarios across the top (p. 83).
- Options that work in every scenario go forward; ones that work in none are revised; ones that work in some
  become contingency plans or are modified (p. 81).
- A single session is "a rough and ready process" (p. 84).
- What to watch for: groupthink, and defensiveness around favoured options (p. 80).

### Three Horizons (p. 42–48)
- The toolkit's workshop runs Horizon One, then Horizon Three, then Horizon Two, then the write-up (p. 44).
- Horizon One: what is taken for granted today, each item placed where it starts to lose its fit (p. 45).
  Horizon Three: what is emerging today and will become common (p. 46). Horizon Two: pair one challenged
  Horizon One feature with the Horizon Three change that challenges it, and list what could be done (p. 46).
- It credits Baghai, Coley and White 1999 for the first version and Sharpe and Hodgson for the adaptation (p. 48).

## What it means for the plugin
- The plugin's playbooks follow the three questions: what is changing (`scan`, `trends`, `base-rates`,
  `horizons`, `drivers`), so what (`layers`, `scenarios`, `wheels`, `forecasts`), now what (`stress-test`,
  `signposts`, `review`). The grouping is the toolkit's; which playbook sits where is this plugin's choice.
- `scan`, `drivers`, `wheels`, `scenarios` and `stress-test` adapt the toolkit's steps. The toolkit writes them for groups: at least 6 people for drivers and wheels, at least 8 to 12 for
  scenarios, up to 16 for stress tests. Here one person works with a model, so the steps that exist to
  compare groups are replaced by independent passes that are then compared. That replacement is this plugin's
  choice and is not tested.
- The toolkit is guidance. It gives steps and warnings, and it says itself that the effect of futures work is
  hard to prove. No rule here claims that a step improves a decision.
