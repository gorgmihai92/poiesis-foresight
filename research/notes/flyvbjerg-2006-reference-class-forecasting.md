# From Nobel Prize to Project Management: Getting Risks Right (Flyvbjerg, 2006)

- **Source:** Project Management Journal 37(3): 5–15 · https://doi.org/10.1177/875697280603700302 (the author's
  copy, arXiv:1302.3642; pages = PDF pages of that copy, 32 in all)
- **File:** `../papers/flyvbjerg-2006-nobel-prize-project-management.pdf` (local only)
- **Licence:** arXiv non-exclusive licence: local only.
- **Evidence:** analytical, with one practical case · it reports forecast errors from the author's earlier studies
  of transport projects and describes the first use of the method (p. 3, 10–14).
- **Read:** part of the text: the abstract, the sections on inaccuracy, its explanations, the method, its first
  use, and the potentials and barriers (p. 2–14 and 16–17), on 2026-11-22. Not read: p. 15, the references,
  tables, figures and endnotes (p. 18–32).

## What it says
- Reference class forecasting is "a method for systematically taking an outside view on planned actions"
  (p. 6). It has three steps: identify a reference class of past, similar projects; establish a probability
  distribution for that class; compare the specific project with the distribution (p. 7).
- The class "must be broad enough to be statistically meaningful but narrow enough to be truly comparable with
  the specific project" (p. 7).
- The method does not try to forecast the specific events that will affect the project. It places the project in
  the distribution of outcomes of the class (p. 7).
- The inside view focuses on the project at hand and its details; the outside view looks at the outcomes of a
  class of similar projects. The paper takes both terms from Kahneman and Tversky (1979) and Lovallo and
  Kahneman (2003) (p. 6, 8).
- A limit the paper states: the outside view "may fail to predict extreme outcomes, that is, those that lie
  outside all historical precedents" (p. 9).
- A second limit: choosing the reference class is harder when precedents are not easily found, for instance for
  new and unfamiliar technologies (p. 9–10).
- The forecast may be moved away from the class's distribution only on evidence: lower uplifts are justified
  "only if" the managers can show they estimate better than those in the reference class did (p. 13).
- The errors it reports are from transport projects: average cost overrun of 44.7% for rail, 33.8% for bridges
  and tunnels, 20.4% for roads; rail passenger forecasts off by −51.4% on average (p. 3). The reference class for
  roads held 172 completed projects (p. 11).
- The first use in practice: the UK Department for Transport and HM Treasury adopted it in 2004 for large
  transport projects (p. 10), and it was applied to the Edinburgh Tram Line 2 in October 2004 (p. 14).
- Where forecasts are bent on purpose (the paper calls it strategic misrepresentation), the method is not wanted
  and needs accountability beside it (p. 16–17).

## What it means for the plugin
- `playbooks/base-rates.md` uses the three steps as they are: a class, its distribution, then the case.
- The class has to be stated with its size. A class too small to count, or a subject with no precedent, is said
  plainly: the base rate is then "not available", not a guess.
- An adjustment away from the base rate names the evidence for it.
- The paper is about the cost and demand of building projects. That the same method helps for the growth of a
  research topic is this plugin's analogy, not the paper's finding. The paper shows the method in use; it is not
  a test of how much it improves accuracy.
