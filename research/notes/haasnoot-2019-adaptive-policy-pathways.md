# Dynamic Adaptive Policy Pathways (DAPP) (Haasnoot, Warren & Kwakkel, 2019)

- **Source:** chapter 4 of Marchau, Walker, Bloemen & Popper (eds.), *Decision Making under Deep Uncertainty: From
  Theory to Practice*, Springer, p. 71–92 · https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-05252-2_4 (the publisher's HTML edition; no
  page numbers, cited by section)
- **File:** none here (the chapter is open to read at the publisher)

- **Evidence:** analytical · a planning method described by its authors, with an illustration on a river model.
  Not a test of whether the method works.
- **Read:** part of the text, on 2026-11-22, in the browser: section 4.2 "The DAPP Approach", 4.4 "Under What

## What it says
- A plan is a series of actions over time. A policy has a design life and may stop meeting its objectives when
  conditions change; that point is its adaptation tipping point (4.2). In step 2 these are "those conditions
  under which a specified indicator fails to meet its target" (4.2, Step 2).
- Seven steps: frame the problem, with objectives as indicators and targets, and name the uncertainties; assess
  vulnerabilities and opportunities and find the tipping points; identify actions and their own tipping points;
  design and evaluate pathways; design the adaptive strategy with its monitoring system; implement; monitor (4.2).
- A tipping point can be stated in absolute terms (this action is enough up to a given level) or in relative
  ones (this action accommodates more change than that one) (4.2, Step 2).
- The monitoring system describes "signposts to monitor and related trigger points that indicate the necessity to
  implement the next actions" (4.2, Step 5).
- A signal should come before the decision point. "‘Timely’ means that there is enough time to have the actions
  performing effectively before an ATP is reached", counting the time to prepare, implement and activate the
  action (4.2, Step 5).
- "‘Reliable’ means no missed signal or false alarm" (4.2, Step 5).
- The signal need not be the thing the objective is stated in. Indicators tied to norms or targets "may not give
  timely and reliable signals", because they often depend on rare extreme conditions; signals can also be found
  in driving forces: trends and events, technology, societal values (4.2, Step 5, reporting Haasnoot et al. 2015).
- A signal can call for more research, preparation, implementation, or a reassessment of the plan (4.2, Steps 6
  and 7).
- Where it fits: when the time to implement is short compared with the rate of change, when decisions can be
  staged, "and when trends can be signalled" (4.4).
- What is open, by the authors' account: "studies into appropriate monitoring signals are very much at a nascent
  stage"; showing practitioners the value of the approach is still a challenge; most work was done for large,
  well-funded national studies (4.7).

## What it means for the plugin
- A signpost row states the lead time: how long the response takes to prepare and to work. A trigger that would
  be reached too late for the response to matter is rewritten or marked as "too late to act on".
- The indicator may be a driver upstream of the outcome, and the row says which it is: the outcome itself, or a
  driver.
- When a trigger is reached, the row's response is one of the kinds the two chapters give. "Look closer" (more
  research) is a valid response and is written as one.
- `playbooks/review.md` reads each signpost against its trigger on a date, and records a missed signal or a false
  alarm when a later review shows one. Those two words are the chapter's own test of a signal.
- The chapter says the study of signals is at an early stage. Nothing here claims a signpost will fire in time.
  The method was built for water management over decades; its use for a small project is this plugin's choice.
